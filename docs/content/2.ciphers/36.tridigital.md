Tridigital turns each letter into one digit. Most digits stand for three letters, so decoding gives you the whole choice in brackets. That's not a failed decode. It's a pattern you can take straight to a word list.

```ts
const tridigital = create("tridigital");
const keys = { key: "NOVELCRAFT", secondKey: "DRAGONFLY" };
tridigital.encode("The ides of March", keys).text; // "03095607958910773"
tridigital.decode("03095 60795 89107 73.", keys).text;
// "[AET][GHU][AET] [OIV][DBQ][AET][RCS] [OIV][FKX] [LMZ][AET][RCS][RCS][GHU]"
```

## The block

Tridigital is an [ACA cipher type](https://www.cryptogram.org/downloads/aca.info/ciphers/Tridigital.pdf). According to [CryptoCrack](https://sites.google.com/site/cryptocrackprogram/user-guide/cipher-types/other/tridigital), ACA member OWLEYES published it in *The Cryptogram* in July-August 1959.

Both `key` and `secondKey` are required. The first sets the digits above a block ten columns wide. Number a ten-letter keyword in alphabetical order, from 1 to 9, then 0 for the tenth letter. Repeated letters are numbered left to right.

The second key builds the alphabet. Drop repeated letters from the keyword, then add the rest of A to Z. `DRAGONFLY` becomes `DRAGONFLYBCEHIJKMPQSTUVWXZ`. You can also supply all 26 letters.

Write that alphabet across the first nine columns, row by row. The tenth column stays empty. Its digit separates words.

```text
    N O V E L C R A F T
    6 7 0 3 5 2 8 1 4 9
    D R A G O N F L Y -
    B C E H I J K M P -
    Q S T U V W X Z - -
```

There are 26 letters in 27 cells, so one letter column has only two. Each letter becomes the digit above its column. A space becomes `9` here.

You can also give `key` as the ten digits in any order. They're used as they stand. `key: "6703528149"` makes the same block as `NOVELCRAFT`. That's useful with sites that number differently. kryptografie.de counts from 0, so pass its digits as they stand.

## What does decoding give you?

A digit can't tell you which letter was meant. The decoder returns its whole column, top row first, as a regex character class. The separator becomes a space. Picking a letter after that is a guess.

Try `grep -xE '[AET][GHU][AET]' words.txt`. It finds THE, but also AGE, AHA and TUT. All four fit. For a candidate sentence, ``new RegExp(`^${decoded}$`)`` works the same way.

Is that good security? No. Tridigital is hopeless as real crypto, but it's a good puzzle. A digit count finds the separator, word lengths fall out, and solvers fit words from a list.

## What it skips

Encoding uppercases the text, keeps A to Z, and splits words on whitespace. Punctuation and digits are dropped. The ciphertext comes out as one run of digits.

Decoding reads only digits. Groups of five and a final period, as in the example above, are fine.

## Checked against

The ACA example, both ways. CryptoCrack's TRAMPOLINE and OSCARWILDE example. Two examples from The Black Chamber blog in 2020: SUNDAY and teacher.

## Errors and analysis

A missing `key` or `secondKey` raises `MissingOptionError`. A `key` that isn't ten ASCII letters or the digits 0 to 9 once each raises `InvalidOptionError`. So does a `secondKey` with no ASCII letter. Decoding text with no digits raises `CipherError`.

[`guess`](/guide/analysis) doesn't know Tridigital. [`recover`](/guide/analysis) doesn't take it.
